A History of the Christian Church · Era VIII

Era VIII · AD 1618–1800

Orthodoxy and Awakening

“Man’s chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever.”

Westminster Shorter Catechism, Q. 1

Reformed churches settle their confessions at Dort and Westminster, and Puritan pastors write deep devotional works. As the Enlightenment questions revelation, revival breaks out across Britain and America.

Confessions are written in an age of religious war. Revival later crosses the borders of state churches.

Opening

The Synod of Dort is the only Synod of a quasi-oecumenical character in the history of the Reformed Churches. In this respect it is even more important than the Westminster Assembly of Divines, which was confined to England and Scotland, although it produced superior doctrinal standards.

Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, vol. I §65. Read the whole

Methodism forms the third great wave of the Evangelical Protestant movement in England, and represents the idea of revival. The Reformation destroyed the power of the papacy. Puritanism aimed at a more thorough Reformation in Church and State, and controlled for a time the civil and religious life of the nation. Methodism kept aloof from politics, and confined itself to the sphere of practical religion.

Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, vol. I §109. Read the whole

If evangelical Christianity to-day has a stronger hold on the Anglo-Saxon race in both hemispheres than on any other nation, it is chiefly due to the influence of Puritanism and Methodism.

People

  • William Twisse 1578–1646 — Prolocutor of the Westminster Assembly
  • Samuel Rutherford 1600–1661 — Minister of Anwoth, Westminster commissioner, writer of letters from his banishment
  • Alexander Henderson 1583–1646 — Drafter of the National Covenant and moderator of the Glasgow Assembly
  • James Guthrie c. 1612–1661 — Minister of Stirling, hanged in Edinburgh after the Restoration
  • Margaret Wilson c. 1667–1685 — Covenanter girl drowned at the stake in the Solway at Wigtown
  • James Renwick 1662–1688 — Field preacher, the last Covenanter put to death
  • Richard Sibbes 1577–1635 — Puritan preacher at Gray’s Inn, writer of The Bruised Reed
  • Thomas Goodwin 1600–1680 — Independent at Westminster, Cromwell's chaplain
  • John Owen 1616–1683 — Puritan theologian, chief author of the Savoy Declaration
  • Richard Baxter 1615–1691 — Pastor of Kidderminster, ejected in 1662
  • John Flavel c. 1627–1691 — Ejected minister of Dartmouth, writer of The Fountain of Life
  • William Kiffin 1616–1701 — London merchant and Particular Baptist pastor
  • John Bunyan 1628–1688 — Tinker and preacher of Bedford, twelve years in prison
  • Benjamin Keach 1640–1704 — Particular Baptist pastor in London, author of the Baptist Catechism
  • Elizabeth Gaunt d. 1685 — Baptist woman of London, burned at Tyburn for sheltering a rebel
  • Cyril Lucar 1572–1638 — Patriarch of Constantinople
  • Blaise Pascal 1623–1662 — Mathematician and defender of Port-Royal
  • Antoine Court 1695–1760 — Restorer of the Reformed church of France after the Revocation
  • Philipp Jakob Spener 1635–1705 — Lutheran pastor, father of Pietism
  • August Hermann Francke 1663–1727 — Professor at Halle, founder of its orphan house
  • Count Zinzendorf 1700–1760 — Patron and bishop of the renewed Moravian Church
  • John Wesley 1703–1791 — Anglican priest and founder of Methodism
  • Charles Wesley 1707–1788 — Hymn writer of the revival
  • George Whitefield 1714–1770 — Calvinistic evangelist in Britain and America
  • Jonathan Edwards 1703–1758 — Pastor of Northampton, then president at Princeton
  • Selina, Countess of Huntingdon 1707–1791 — Patron of Whitefield and of chapels
  • John Gill 1697–1771 — Particular Baptist pastor and commentator in London
  • Andrew Fuller 1754–1815 — Pastor at Kettering, first secretary of the Baptist mission
  • William Carey 1761–1834 — Shoemaker, pastor and missionary to India
