Era VI · AD 1294–1517

The Late Middle Ages

“At the day of judgment we shall not be asked what we have read, but what we have done.”

Thomas à Kempis, The Imitation of Christ 1.3

Popes move to Avignon and then split into rival lines. Wycliffe and Hus appeal to Scripture against the hierarchy, Constantinople falls to the Turks, and printing and the new learning prepare the ground for reform.

A worldly papacy and a hungry laity. Reform is demanded from councils, kings and preachers.

Opening

The two centuries intervening between 1294 and 1517, between the accession of Boniface VIII. and the nailing of Luther’s Ninety-five Theses against the church door in Wittenberg, mark the gradual transition from the Middle Ages to modern times, from the universal acceptance of the papal theocracy in Western Europe to the assertion of national independence, from the supreme authority of the priesthood to the intellectual and spiritual freedom of the individual. Old things are passing away; signs of a new order increase. Institutions are seen to be breaking up. The scholastic systems of theology lose their compulsive hold on men’s minds, and even become the subject of ridicule. The abuses of the earlier Middle Ages call forth voices demanding reform on the basis of the Scriptures and the common well-being of mankind. The inherent vital energies in the Church seek expression in new forms of piety and charitable deed.

David S. Schaff, History of the Christian Church, vol. VI §1. Read the whole

The religious unrest and the passion for a better state of affairs found expression in Wyclif, Huss, and other leaders who, by their clear apprehension of truth and readiness to stand by their public utterances, even unto death, stood far above their own age and have shone in all the ages since. While coarse ambition and nepotism, a total perversion of the ecclesiastical office and violation of the fundamental virtues of the Christian life held rule in the highest place of Christendom, a pure stream of piety was flowing in the Church of the North, and the mystics along the Rhine and in the Lowlands were unconsciously fertilizing the soil from which the Reformation was to spring forth.

People

  • Boniface VIII c. 1235–1303 — Pope of Unam Sanctam
  • Marsiglius of Padua c. 1275–1342 — Author of the Defender of the Peace
  • Catherine of Siena 1347–1380 — Mystic who urged the pope back to Rome
  • John Gerson 1363–1429 — Chancellor of Paris at Constance
  • Julian of Norwich c. 1343–after 1416 — Anchoress, wrote the Revelations of Divine Love
  • John Wyclif c. 1324–1384 — Oxford teacher, first to give the English the Bible
  • Gerard Groote 1340–1384 — Preacher, father of the Brothers of the Common Life
  • John Hus c. 1372–1415 — Preacher of Prague, burned at Constance
  • Thomas à Kempis 1380–1471 — Canon of Mount St. Agnes, The Imitation of Christ
  • Wessel Gansfort 1420–1489 — Reformer before the Reformation
  • Lorenzo Valla 1407–1457 — Humanist who exposed the Donation of Constantine
  • Girolamo Savonarola 1452–1498 — Dominican preacher of Florence
  • Julius II 1443–1513 — The warrior pope
  • Erasmus c. 1466–1536 — Humanist, editor of the Greek New Testament
