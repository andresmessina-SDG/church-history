A history of the Christian church

Sources, Credits and Method

“Wherefore seeing we also are compassed about with so great a cloud of witnesses, let us lay aside every weight… and let us run with patience the race that is set before us.”Hebrews 12:1

The witnesses. The story is told, as far as it can be, in the words of those who lived it and of the historians who studied them: Scripture, the fathers, the councils and creeds, the Reformers and their heirs. Each passage is quoted from a public-domain edition, its author named in red above it and its place given in the margin.

The narrator. Philip Schaff’s History of the Christian Church carries the story to the Reformation, with his Creeds of Christendom for the confessions. Where Schaff ends, George Park Fisher and the writings of each age take up the thread. After 1930, where texts are still in copyright, only short excerpts are quoted, each with its credit.

The editor’s words. Words not quoted from a source are the editor’s. They are kept few and set apart, to join the witnesses, not to speak over them. Where later scholarship has corrected a detail in the older histories, the present reading is given without comment.

The standpoint. The history is written from the Reformed faith, on Schaff’s foundation, and gives the Particular Baptists their place. The church is known by its marks, the pure preaching of the gospel and the right use of the sacraments, and not by an unbroken line of office (Belgic Confession 27–29; Westminster Confession 25). No single body runs straight down the river: Christ’s one church flows through every channel where his gospel is.

The river. After 1517 the channels follow the confessions as Schaff sets them out in the third volume of his Creeds. The gold in the water marks where the gospel was confessed and recovered, as far as the record shows. It does not claim to show who was saved.

The pictures. No picture shows God or Christ (Heidelberg Catechism 96–98; Westminster Larger Catechism 109). Gold is kept for the words of Scripture.
